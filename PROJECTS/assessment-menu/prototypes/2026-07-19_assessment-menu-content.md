## Overview

At Cardiff University, effective assessment is guided by principles that help ensure tasks are meaningful, inclusive, and aligned with what we want students to know and be able to do. As you explore this menu, consider how your assessment design supports authenticity, sustainability, the balance of assessment for/as/of learning, responsible use of AI, and academic integrity.

This menu is not a list of recommended replacements. It is a design tool to support thoughtful choices about assessment purpose, authenticity, inclusivity, and the role of AI across a programme.

## 1. Exams

Assessment types in this category:

- In-person, invigilated exam
- Open-book exam

### In-person, invigilated exam

#### What is it?

An in‑person exam is a time‑limited, supervised assessment completed on campus, where students work individually under defined conditions and respond to unseen or partially seen tasks.

#### What's it for?

- Providing secure evidence of individual learning
- Evaluating conceptual understanding
- Assessing judgement
- Used at key points of progression within a programme

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Lane 1, in‑person exams validate programme learning outcomes under supervised conditions, where students work independently and without reliance on GenAI, but Lane 2 designs make ethical, critical GenAI use explicit rather than hidden.

Consider:

- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Use invigilated, supervised conditions
- Require students to work independently, without GenAI (unless explicitly permitted)
- Focus on programme‑level learning outcomes
- Design questions that assess reasoning and understanding, not memorisation alone
- Apply clear, standardised marking criteria

###### Lane 1 Example

A two-hour, invigilated, closed-book exam in which students answer structured conceptual questions, explain disciplinary theories or methods, and apply taught procedures to unseen problems.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- In‑person exams can be designed to incorporate and scaffold GenAI use, supporting students to develop ethical, critical, and reflective AI practices while still working within time‑limited, campus‑based conditions.
- Scaffold GenAI use as part of the assessment design
- Make students' interaction with AI visible and assessable
- Focus on evaluation, judgement, critique, or adaptation rather than generation alone
- Include explicit prompts requiring students to justify or reflect on AI use

###### Lane 2 Example

An in‑person exam where students bring AI‑generated notes or feedback produced through a structured pre‑exam activity, and during the exam critically evaluate, adapt, or apply this material to an unseen task, including a short reflection on the value and limits of GenAI.

#### Authenticity

Exams are often characterised as a traditional form of assessment that offers limited scope for the meaningful application of knowledge beyond recall and does not align with how learning is typically used in academic or professional contexts. While they may test students' ability to perform under pressure, their contribution to cognitive authenticity is limited and highly dependent on careful question design. For this reason, exams are most defensible when used selectively at programme level, with tasks that prioritise interpretation, evaluative judgement, and decision‑making under constraint rather than speed or memorisation.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Inclusive design in exams helps ensure that all students can demonstrate their knowledge and abilities fairly, without unnecessary barriers created by format, timing, or cognitive load.

Inclusive design considerations:

- Use clear and well-structured questions
- Ensure appropriate cognitive and reading load
- Provide advance information about exam format
- Avoid unnecessary emphasis on speeded performance

Reasonable adjustments:

- Allow extra time where appropriate
- Offer rest breaks
- Provide alternative formats
- Enable the use of assistive technologies

#### Student Voice

"it's really stressful to have your whole grade depending on that one exam"

### Open-book exam

#### What is it?

An open‑book exam is a time‑limited assessment in which tasks are completed with access to specified resources (e.g. notes, readings, data, or online materials), typically under supervised or semi‑supervised conditions.

#### What's it for?

- Assessing students' ability to apply, interpret, and synthesise knowledge
- Conducted under time constraints
- Requires independent responses to unseen prompts
- Focuses on analysis rather than recall of information

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Open‑book exams require careful design because access to resources means GenAI use is difficult to restrict and should therefore be explicitly addressed or incorporated.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Run the exam under in‑person, supervised conditions
- Restrict GenAI use explicitly and communicate this clearly
- Design questions that require reasoned responses grounded in course‑specific materials, not generic explanations
- Emphasise interpretation and explanation over searching

###### Lane 1 Example

An invigilated open‑book exam where students access approved notes and texts and respond to unseen questions that require explanation of concepts using specific course materials.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Scaffold the use of GenAI as part of the assessment
- Make students' use of resources and AI visible and accountable
- Design tasks that require evaluation, synthesis, critique, or adaptation, not content generation
- Include prompts requiring students to reflect on or justify how AI was used

###### Lane 2 Example

A time‑limited exam where students may use notes and GenAI tools to support planning or sense‑making but are assessed on their ability to critically evaluate information, apply it to a prompt, and explain the role and limitations of AI in their response.

#### Authenticity

Open‑book exams are often assumed to promote authenticity, but in practice may still assess recall or searching unless carefully designed. Their contribution to cognitive authenticity depends on tasks that require students to interpret information, weigh alternatives, and exercise judgement, rather than locate or reproduce content from available resources.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Open book formats can reduce some barriers associated with memory-based assessment but may still disadvantage students if expectations around question demands, resource use, and time constraints are unclear.

Inclusive design strategies:

- Provide transparent guidance on permitted materials and AI use
- Ensure manageable reading and resource expectations
- Use predictable structures and question formats

Reasonable adjustments:

- Allow extra time where appropriate
- Offer alternative formats
- Enable the use of assistive technologies

## 2. Class Tests

Assessment types in this category:

- Timed in-class knowledge test (short answer and MCQs)
- Application-Based Problem Test
- Mentimeter Conceptual Understanding Check

### Timed in-class knowledge test (short answer and MCQs)

#### What is it?

A short, time-limited test taken during a scheduled class, typically combining multiple-choice questions and brief written responses to assess foundational knowledge

#### What's it for?

- Checking students' grasp of core concepts and terminology
- Providing early diagnostic feedback to students and staff
- Encouraging regular engagement with content

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

If delivered in person, this type of in-class test is very low risk in terms of AI usage as it is supervised.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Conduct under direct supervision by a tutor
- Ensure controlled conditions, which will enhance academic integrity

###### Lane 1 Example

In a first-year module, students attend a scheduled session where they complete a 60-minute multiple-choice and short-answer test in a classroom. A tutor is present throughout to invigilate, ensuring students work independently and adhere to assessment rules. The questions are standardised and completed under timed conditions, allowing all students to be assessed consistently.

This supervised, controlled setup clearly aligns with lane 1 assessment, where the emphasis is on direct oversight and maintaining academic integrity.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

To align with lane 2, this assessment could incorporate greater student autonomy, for example through:

- use of open-book formats.
- flexible timing
- reduced invigilation

###### Lane 2 Example

Students attend a scheduled session and complete a short test in a computer lab within a 60-minute timeframe, but with access to notes or online resources and minimal invigilation (e.g. one tutor overseeing multiple groups). While it is timed and takes place in a classroom setting, the reduced supervision and open-resource format mean it sits more appropriately within lane 2.

#### Authenticity

Authenticity in in-class tests can be maintained by designing questions that require application, analysis, or problem-solving in a supervised environment, so students have to draw on their own knowledge and skills rather than rely on memorised answers or external support.

Practical ways to do this:

- Use scenario-based or problem-solving questions
- Include short written justifications/explanations, not just recall
- Vary questions each year to avoid predictability
- This keeps the assessment both controlled and meaningful, rather than just a memory test.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Inclusive design in class tests ensures that all students can demonstrate their knowledge without being disadvantaged by avoidable barriers.

Extra time or rest breaks as required – time pressure may disadvantage some students

Alternative formats (eg. Screen-readable papers)

Clear instructions and consistent structure to reduce cognitive load

Question wording should avoid unnecessary complexity

### Application-Based Problem Test

#### What is it?

An in-class test requiring students to apply knowledge to unseen problems, case studies, or data sets.

#### What's it for?

- Assessing application and reasoning
- Evaluating problem-solving skills

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

This particular assessment type has strong protection against AI misuse due to unseen, contextual questions delivered in person.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Create an in-class, supervised test where students apply knowledge to unseen problems
- Controlled conditions to demonstrate independent learning.

###### Lane 1 Example

Students complete a timed, invigilated case-based test where they diagnose a problem and justify their answer without external support.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Open, take-home assessment where students apply knowledge to complex or unfamiliar problems.

###### Lane 2 Example

Students analyse a real-world data set and produce a report interpreting trends and proposing solutions, drawing on course concepts and external tools where appropriate.

#### Authenticity

Students value tests that reflect 'real' problem solving rather than memorisation. Application-based problem tests allow learners to demonstrate understanding by applying concepts to practical, often unfamiliar scenarios, mirroring how knowledge is used in real-world contexts. This approach not only promotes deeper learning but also better prepares students for professional and academic challenges beyond the classroom.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Application-based problem tests should be designed to support students in demonstrating their reasoning as well as their final answers, by structuring tasks in ways that make expectations clear and manageable. Think about:

- Providing step-by-step scaffolding within questions
- Avoiding overloading a single test with too many tasks
- Ensuring that expectations around method and working are made explicit

### Mentimeter Conceptual Understanding Check

#### What is it?

A diagnostic or formative class test using Mentimeter. This would be a live, in-class diagnostic activity rather than a formal assessment method, using Mentimeter to gather students' responses to conceptual, reflective or application-based questions.

#### What's it for?

- Checking conceptual understanding
- Supporting dialogue and peer learning
- Identifying threshold concepts and misunderstandings

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

In-class Mentimeter activities typically limit unsupervised AI use, supporting more authentic insight into student thinking. When used outside class (e.g. asynchronous polls), students may use AI to help formulate responses, which can be leveraged as a learning support tool rather than restricted.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Run Mentimeter questions live in a timetabled session
- Require students to respond individually to unseen questions without external tools.

###### Lane 1 Example

In a Cardiff University biosciences lecture, students respond to a series of concept-check questions about a newly introduced process (e.g. cellular respiration stages). Responses are displayed in real time and used to clarify misconceptions immediately.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Extend Mentimeter activities beyond the live classroom (e.g. pre/post-session or asynchronous tasks)
- Encourage students to reflect, discuss, and potentially use digital tools (including AI) to refine their thinking.

###### Lane 2 Example

Students in a Cardiff Business School module respond to an open-ended Mentimeter question before class (e.g. "What factors influence consumer trust in a digital economy?"). In class, responses are analysed collectively, with students comparing perspectives and building on ideas.

#### Authenticity

This method mirrors professional practices such as polling and consultation. It encourages students to articulate uncertainty as a normal part of learning.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

When using Mentimeter for in-class tests, inclusive design involves ensuring that all students can engage meaningfully by providing flexible participation options, clear and accessible question design, and appropriate adjustments to accommodate diverse needs and digital access. Think about:

- Providing alternative ways to participate (e.g. verbal contribution, written submission if digital access is limited).
- Ensuring questions are clearly worded and accessible.
- Allowing additional time or repetition for students who need it.
- Being mindful of digital accessibility (screen readers, device access, connectivity).

## 3. Portfolios

Assessment types in this category:

- Reflective Portfolio

### Reflective Portfolio

#### What is it?

A curated collection of reflections (written, audio or multimodal), evidencing learning over time, often linked to theory, practice and personal development.

#### What's it for?

- Demonstrating learning progression across the programme
- Encouraging integration of theory and experience
- Supporting metacognition, reflection and critical thinking

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

AI has the ability to generate summaries of readings, suggest reflective prompts or structures, help draft or edit reflections and support idea generation or connections to theory. There is a risk that over-reliance may reduce authenticity and personal voice, but there is an opportunity in that AI can support deeper reflection if used critically.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Have students complete reflections in class under controlled conditions

###### Lane 1 Example

A lane 1 example of a reflective portfolio would be anything created under controlled conditions in an observed environment.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Reflective portfolios are inherently Lane 2 as they are typically completed outside controlled conditions, meaning educators cannot directly observe or regulate student use of AI tools.
- Depth of reflection exposes over-reliance on AI, as weak, generic, or overly polished responses often signal limited engagement, whereas strong work demonstrates nuanced personal reflection.
- Strong performance depends on authentic experience, and students must draw on their own observed practice, decisions, and development, making it difficult to rely solely on AI-generated content.
- However, they resist superficial AI use. High-quality reflective work requires personal, contextualised, and specific insight, which generic AI outputs struggle to replicate convincingly.
- AI use is difficult to monitor in this format because portfolios involve extended, independent writing over time, it is not feasible to fully track how or when AI has been used in their production.

###### Lane 2 Example

A Lane 2 reflective portfolio might include a student linking their experience of facilitating a seminar to theories of teaching and student engagement. The reflection moves beyond personal experience to demonstrate critical engagement with literature, using theory to interpret and evaluate practice. Even if AI supported the identification of sources or structuring, the quality of the work depends on the student's ability to meaningfully apply and critique these ideas.

#### Authenticity

This may provide students with an opportunity to create a professional portfolio, or a collection of work that may be presented to prospective clients and employers; students may gain a wide range of transferable skills.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Consider:

Offering flexible formats:

- Written, audio, video, visual portfolios
- Scaffolded reflection prompts

Option to submit:

- Bullet-point reflections
- Recorded reflections
- Mind maps

Providing:

- Clear examples of both lanes
- Templates and guided questions
- Accessibility considerations (e.g., captions, alt text)

### Annotated Bibliography

#### What is it?

A structured list of sources (such as books, journal articles, and websites), where each reference is accompanied by a short annotation. The annotation typically summarises the source, evaluates its credibility or relevance and explains how it relates to a particular topic or research question.

#### What's it for?

- Developing research and information literacy skills
- Promoting summarising, analysing, and synthesising information
- Helping students build a foundation for essays, projects, or dissertations
- Supporting understanding of academic conversations within a subject area
- Encouraging critical evaluation of sources

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

There is significant potential for students to use AI tools to generate summaries or annotations of sources. This introduces a risk that learners may rely on AI without fully engaging with the original texts or developing their own evaluative skills. However, AI can also be used constructively to support understanding, for example by helping to clarify complex texts or suggest initial summaries. It is therefore important to design tasks that require critical engagement, verification of information, and clear demonstration of the student's own interpretation.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Include in-class elements or supervised tasks
- Require links between annotations and taught content or discussions
- Ask for evidence of reading (e.g., quotations, specific points)

###### Lane 1 Example

A Lane 1 annotated bibliography under controlled conditions might involve a student completing their annotations during a supervised session (e.g. in-class or timed assessment) where AI use is either restricted or explicitly disclosed and visible to the examiner. For example, the student could annotate a source immediately after an observed teaching activity, explaining how its ideas relate to what they actually did and what was observed, with any AI use (eg. for summarising the article) clearly documented in real time. This ensures the examiner can see both the student's authentic thinking and exactly how AI was used, making the work verifiably grounded in personal, observed practice rather than generated content.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Allow AI use for initial summarising or note-taking
- Require students to check, refine, and critique AI-generated content
- Include a reflective element on how AI was used
- Emphasise synthesis, comparison, and originality in annotations
- Ask students to identify inaccuracies or gaps in AI outputs

###### Lane 2 Example

A Lane 2 annotated bibliography example would involve a student independently producing annotations outside controlled conditions, where AI use may support tasks like summarising sources or identifying key arguments. For instance, a student might annotate a paper on formative assessment by summarising its main claims (potentially AI-assisted) and then critically evaluating its relevance to higher education practice. The quality of the work depends on how well the student engages with, critiques, and applies the literature, meaning that simply relying on AI-generated summaries would not achieve high marks without clear evidence of understanding and analysis.

#### Authenticity

Authenticity can be supported by requiring students to demonstrate clear, traceable engagement with their chosen sources. This might include referencing specific arguments, using brief quotations, or linking sources directly to module content or assessment tasks. Asking students to explain why each source was selected, how it informed their understanding, or how it connects to their own research focus can help foreground their individual thinking. Incorporating reflective commentary or requiring links to subsequent coursework (e.g., essays or projects) further strengthens authenticity by situating the bibliography within the student's own learning journey.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Annotated bibliographies can be flexibly designed to support diverse learners. Consider:

- Offering a choice of sources and formats (eg. written, audio, or video annotations), or annotation length to suit different needs and preferences.
- Providing clear scaffolding such as templates, exemplars, and guided questions to reduce cognitive load and support students unfamiliar with academic conventions.
- Allowing the use of digital tools, including AI for initial comprehension support, to enhance accessibility, providing that expectations around critical engagement and academic integrity are clearly communicated.

Reasonable adjustments might include:

- Extended deadlines
- Alternative formats
- Assistive technology use (eg. text-to-speech, speech-to-text technology).

### Professional Portfolio

#### What is it?

A professional portfolio is a curated collection of work that demonstrates a student's skills, knowledge, and development over time. It may include artefacts such as project work, reflections, case studies, presentations, or evidence from placements, typically accompanied by commentary that explains the context, learning, and progression.

#### What's it for?

- Demonstrating applied skills and professional competencies
- Promotes self-directed learning and goal setting
- Builds a body of work that can be used for employment or further study
- Supports integration of theory and practice
- Encouraging reflection on learning and development over time

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Professional portfolios can operate across both lanes, depending on how they are designed and assessed, balancing authentic, open-ended learning with opportunities to verify individual achievement.

There is significant potential for students to use AI tools to support drafting, reflection, or presentation within a portfolio. This introduces a risk that outputs may not fully represent the student's own understanding or experience. However, AI can also support learning by helping students organise ideas, improve clarity, or reflect more deeply on their work. It is therefore important to design portfolio tasks that emphasise personalisation, critical reflection, and clear links to individual experience, ensuring that students demonstrate ownership of their work and decision-making processes.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Include in-person or supervised reflective discussions (e.g. viva, presentation, or portfolio defence)
- Use targeted questioning to verify authenticity and depth of understanding
- Focus assessment on personal experience, decision-making, and reasoning
- Require students to explain and justify their artefacts and learning in real time

###### Lane 1 Example

Students submit a professional portfolio and participate in a short, live oral discussion where they explain selected artefacts, reflect on their learning, and respond to questions from the assessor. Marks are awarded based on the quality of the portfolio and the student's ability to articulate and justify their work in real time.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Allow AI use to support drafting, structuring, and reflection
- Ask students to demonstrate progression over time and connect artefacts
- Emphasise personalisation, synthesis, and integration of experiences
- Include a reflective commentary on how tools (including AI) were used
- Require students to critically evaluate and refine AI-supported outputs

###### Lane 2 Example

Students develop a professional portfolio over the course of a module, drawing on coursework, practical experiences, and reflections. They may use AI tools to help organise and draft reflective commentary but are required to critically review and personalise their work, including a reflection on how their portfolio represents their development and how any tools were used in its creation. Assessment focuses on the coherence, depth, and authenticity of the portfolio.

#### Authenticity

Authenticity can be supported by requiring students to base their portfolio on personal experiences, original work, and individual development. This may include linking artefacts to specific learning activities, placements, or projects, and explaining the rationale behind their inclusion. Reflective commentary that connects experiences, demonstrates progression, and articulates learning helps foreground the student's own voice. Incorporating elements such as oral defences, interim submissions, or links to other assessed work further strengthens confidence that the portfolio represents genuine student achievement.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Consider:

- Offering flexible formats (e.g. written, audio, video, or multimodal portfolios) to suit different strengths and needs
- Providing clear scaffolding, such as templates, examples, and guiding questions to support structure and expectations
- Allowing adjustments to timing, format, and tools, including assistive technologies and staged submissions to reduce cognitive load

Reasonable adjustments might include:

- Extended deadlines
- Alternative formats
- Assistive technology use (eg. text-to-speech, speech-to-text technology).

### Artefacts

#### What is it?

Artefact-based assessment involves students creating tangible or digital outputs that demonstrate their understanding, skills, and application of knowledge. These outputs might include sculptures, paintings, architectural designs, engineering prototypes, or other designed objects. Artefacts often combine conceptual thinking with practical execution, requiring students to synthesise ideas, techniques, and disciplinary knowledge into a coherent product. This type of assessment emphasises process as well as outcome, encouraging iterative development, reflection, and problem-solving.

#### What's it for?

- Assessing applied, practice-based learning and technical skills
- Supporting portfolio-building for future employment or study
- Developing problem-solving and iterative design processes
- Enabling students to demonstrate knowledge through making and doing
- Encouraging creativity, innovation and design thinking

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Producing artefacts can now be susceptible to the misuse of generative AI; encouraging students to demonstrate their ability to use AI tools effectively and critically, and to generate creative solutions can be supported by staff.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Require artefact production to take place in a supervised or observed setting (e.g., studio, lab, workshop)
- Provide structured guidance during observed sessions
- Focus assessment on both the process (observed) and the final product
- Incorporate viva-style questioning or discussion alongside artefact creation
- Limit or control access to external tools during observed periods (including AI where appropriate)
- Use checkpoints where students explain decisions or processes in person
- Include real-time demonstrations of skills and techniques
- Break the task into live or timetabled sessions where progress is monitored

###### Lane 1 Example

In a computer science module, students are required to develop a simple software application during timetabled lab sessions. The task is completed in a supervised environment where students write code using specified tools and platforms. Across a series of structured sessions, they build the application step by step (eg. interface design, core functionality, testing).

Tutors observe students as they work, checking their coding practices, asking questions about their logic, and supporting debugging in real time. Students may also be asked to explain sections of their code or modify it live to demonstrate understanding. The final artefact is assessed alongside the observed development process and a brief in-person explanation of design choices.

This observed approach ensures that the submitted artefact reflects the student's own technical ability and problem-solving skills, while allowing tutors to verify understanding as it develops.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Set an artefact brief to be completed independently outside of supervised sessions
- Focus assessment on final artefact quality, creativity, and evidenced process
- Allow appropriate and transparent use of digital tools, including AI where relevant
- Use process evidence to support academic integrity (rather than observation)
- Incorporate checkpoints such as draft submissions or progress uploads
- Require submission of a reflective commentary or design rationale
- Encourage students to document their process (e.g., photos, videos, journals, portfolios)
- Allow flexibility in when and where the artefact is produced

###### Lane 2 Example

In an architecture module, students design and produce a scale model or digital prototype independently over several weeks. They work off-site and at their own pace, using a range of tools and materials. To support the process, they submit a digital portfolio documenting sketches, iterations, and development stages, alongside a written rationale explaining their design decisions. The final artefact is assessed in combination with this supporting evidence. This approach allows flexibility and creative freedom while still ensuring that the student's work can be authenticated through their documented process and reflection.

#### Authenticity

Artefact-based assessment offers strong authenticity by mirroring the kinds of outputs and processes used in professional practice. Students engage in activities such as producing sculptures, paintings, architectural designs, and engineering models, which reflect real-world disciplinary expectations. This prepares students for practical applications beyond the classroom and helps them develop transferable skills such as design thinking, technical execution, and creative problem-solving. It also contributes to the development of the graduate attribute of being innovative, enterprising, and commercially aware, as students learn to produce work that has potential value in professional or public contexts.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Inclusive design for artefact-based assessment involves anticipating a wide range of student needs, abilities, and resources when setting tasks. While making and designing can be highly engaging, it can also present barriers related to access to materials, space, or technical skills.

Some considerations include:

- Providing alternative formats for artefact submission (e.g., physical or digital versions)
- Ensuring access to necessary materials, tools, or software
- Offering training or support for required technical skills
- Allowing flexibility in scale, complexity, or medium
- Supporting varied levels of digital literacy through guidance and resources
- Using inclusive assessment criteria that value process as well as final product
- Providing clear instructions and exemplars
- Incorporating assistive technologies where appropriate
- Allowing additional time or adapted processes where needed
- Encouraging collaborative or supported approaches where appropriate

Digital literacy can vary, so tasks requiring advanced technology may create accessibility challenges. However, assistive and creative technologies can also enhance participation and open up new possibilities for learners, supporting more inclusive and accessible engagement for all.

- Developing presentation and discussion skills
- Enabling peer learning through sharing and exhibition
- Encouraging synthesis and prioritisation of key information
- Supporting visual representation of complex ideas or data

##### AI Considerations

Producing posters can now be susceptible to the misuse of generative AI; encouraging students to demonstrate their ability to use AI tools effectively and critically, and to generate creative solutions can be supported by staff.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Require posters to be presented in a live, supervised session (e.g., poster conference, exhibition, or viva)
- Provide guidance and rehearsal opportunities prior to the event
- Assess both the poster and the student's live communication skills
- Use questioning to probe depth of understanding
- Observe students discussing and justifying their design and content decisions
- Include a verbal explanation or Q&A component alongside the poster

###### Lane 1 Example

In a health sciences module, students create a research-informed poster on a public health issue and present it at a scheduled poster conference. During the session, each student stands by their poster and responds to questions from tutors and peers about their topic, data interpretation, and design choices. Tutors assess not only the clarity and accuracy of the poster but also the student's ability to explain their work and engage in discussion. This observed format ensures the authenticity of the work and allows direct evaluation of communication skills in a real-time context.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Allow students to design and produce posters independently outside of supervised sessions
- Assess based on clarity, design effectiveness, and supporting explanation
- Encourage appropriate and transparent use of digital tools, including AI
- Use staged submissions (e.g., draft, final poster)
- Provide flexible guidance on format, tools, and style
- Ask students to document their design and development process
- Include a recorded presentation, audio explanation, or written rationale
- Require submission of a digital version of the poster

###### Lane 2 Example

In a business module, students design a poster analysing a contemporary market trend. They complete the work independently over several weeks, using digital tools to create a professional-quality output. Alongside the poster, they submit a short recorded presentation explaining their key insights and design decisions. Some students also include evidence of drafts and feedback to demonstrate their development process. The assessment focuses on the clarity of communication, depth of analysis, and effectiveness of visual design, supported by the student's explanation.

#### Authenticity

Poster assessments offer an authentic way for students to develop and demonstrate communication skills alongside their core subject knowledge. In many professional contexts, individuals are required to present complex ideas in clear, visually engaging formats, whether in conferences, workplaces, or public communication settings. Poster presentations also create opportunities for students to engage with diverse audiences and receive immediate feedback, helping them refine their thinking and communication. This directly supports the development of the graduate attribute of being an effective communicator, as students learn to tailor messages, respond to questions, and present information with clarity and impact.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Inclusive design for poster assessments involves recognising that visually dominant formats can present barriers for some learners, while also offering opportunities for flexible and creative expression. Designing assessments that allow multiple ways to access and demonstrate learning can help ensure equity.

Some considerations include:

- Providing alternative formats (e.g., written report, audio presentation, or narrated slides) where appropriate
- Ensuring digital posters are compatible with screen readers and accessible formats
- Encouraging use of high-contrast colours, clear fonts, and accessible layouts
- Offering guidance on inclusive design principles for visual communication
- Allowing students to submit explanatory text or audio alongside visual elements
- Providing templates to support structure and clarity
- Supporting development of digital skills where needed
- Allowing flexibility in tools and software used to create posters
- Offering additional time or adapted formats where required

Relying solely on a visual format can be challenging for those with visual impairments, so it is important to ensure that equivalent, non-visual ways of demonstrating learning are available. Alternative versions of the assessment, with adapted marking criteria where necessary, can help ensure that all students have equitable opportunities to succeed.

### Blog/Vlog

#### What is it?

A blog or vlog is a reflective or informative piece of content created by a learner, either in written form (blog) or as a recorded video (vlog). It typically communicates ideas, demonstrates understanding, or reflects on learning in a personal and often conversational style.

#### What's it for?

- Used for low-stakes assessment, and for informal collaborative discussion.
- Useful for documenting learning journeys or responding to prompts
- Enables sharing of ideas with peers or wider audiences
- Supports creativity and personal voice
- Develops communication skills (written or verbal)
- Encourages reflection and critical thinking

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

There is a clear potential for students to use AI to generate scripts or write blog content, which may reduce the originality of their work. This creates a risk of over-reliance on AI, particularly for generating ideas or structuring responses, rather than developing these skills independently. However, AI can also serve as a valuable support tool when used appropriately, for example in brainstorming, refining writing, or improving accessibility. It is therefore important to design activities in ways that maintain and foreground the authenticity of the learner's own voice, experiences, and reflections.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Students write or record in controlled or in-class conditions where possible

###### Lane 1 Example

In class, create a 3-minute vlog reflecting on your experience of this week's group activity, including challenges you personally faced and how you addressed them. Refer to at least two moments from the session.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Allow use of AI for planning, structuring, or editing
- Include tasks that combine AI input with original insights
- Encourage transparency in how AI was used
- Require students to evaluate or critique AI outputs
- Focus assessment on critical engagement, synthesis, and personalisation

###### Lane 2 Example

Produce a blog post explaining a key concept from this module. You may use AI tools to help structure your post, but you must:

- Critically evaluate the accuracy of any AI-generated content
- Add your own examples or interpretations
- Include a short note describing how you used AI in your process.

#### Authenticity

A blog/vlog may help students develop a sense of identity and engagement with their discipline, which may help them work towards a sense of professional identity in their chosen field; good practice is allowing an unlimited number of submissions to a forum, with peer as well as tutor feedback through comments, and selection of the best piece for summative assessment.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

If blogs/vlogs are posted on an open forum, there is potential for peer plagiarism.

Since blogs are shorter pieces of writing than essays, and language style can be informal, blogs and vlogs can be useful, low-stakes assessments that get students used to the writing or reflection process, and can help to create communities of learning. As with reports, clarify writing structure and style for those not familiar with this type of writing.

## 5. Oral & Spoken

Assessment types in this category:

- Assessed Seminars
- Presentation
- Viva Voce

### Assessed Seminars

#### What is it?

Students' individual contributions to a seminar are assessed, including interactions with peers, and evidence of preparation, as well as the quality of arguments.

#### What's it for?

- Evaluating students' ability to engage critically and collaboratively with course material
- Supporting learning through dialogue and exposure to diverse perspectives
- Promoting consistent preparation and engagement with seminar readings and topics
- Encouraging active listening and constructive interaction with peers
- Developing and evidencing skills in argumentation, reasoning, and defending ideas
- Assessing communication skills, including clarity, confidence, and responsiveness in discussion

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Assessed seminars align with Lane 1 when they require students to demonstrate their knowledge, reasoning, and communication skills in real time, under conditions that ensure the work is their own. Students may rely on genAI in a remote setting to create pre-prepared scripts on a given topic ahead of the assessment.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Keep assessment live and in-class (or synchronous)
- Assess contributions made in real time during the seminar
- Use follow-up prompts to probe depth of understanding
- Ensure each student contributes verbally or interactively
- Use structured turn-taking, small-group reporting, or directed questions
- Make clear that contributions should reflect students' own reasoning in the moment
- If preparation is allowed, ensure assessment is based on live performance

###### Lane 1 Example

Students participate in a live, in-person seminar where they are assessed on their contributions to a structured academic discussion. Each student is expected to respond to prompts, engage with peers' ideas, and articulate and defend their arguments in real time. The tutor may ask follow-up questions to probe understanding and verify reasoning, with marks awarded for clarity, use of evidence, and responsiveness within the session.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Allow and scaffold the use of AI tools as part of preparation (e.g. idea generation, summarising readings, developing arguments)
- Use seminars as a space to test, refine, and extend ideas developed outside class
- Make expectations explicit around ethical and effective AI use
- Incorporate collaborative activities where students build on and critique ideas (including those generated with AI)
- Encourage students to bring AI-supported notes or prompts into the discussion
- Assess the process as well as the contribution, including preparation, development of ideas, and critical engagement
- Include pre- or post-seminar tasks where students reflect on how they used AI

###### Lane 2 Example

Students prepare for the seminar by using a range of resources, including generative AI tools, to explore key topics and develop initial arguments. During the session, they engage in discussion, drawing on their preparation to contribute ideas, respond to peers, and refine their thinking collaboratively. Assessment considers both the quality of their contributions and their ability to critically engage with and reflect on how their ideas were developed, including the use of AI in their preparation.

#### Authenticity

Seminars develop students' communication skills and the ability to answer questions through group discussion. It provides opportunities for students to demonstrate in-depth knowledge on an element of the curriculum. Seminars help develop students to work towards the graduate attribute; 'independent and critical thinkers' (https://sites.cardiff.ac.uk/education-development-toolkit/theme-overview/employability/graduate-attributes/).

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Inclusive design in oral seminar-style assessments helps ensure that all students can participate meaningfully, regardless of differences in communication style, confidence, or access needs. Anticipating reasonable adjustments and providing flexible modes of engagement can reduce barriers while maintaining the value of discussion-based learning.

Inclusive design considerations:

- Recognise potential challenges for students with anxiety, autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, hearing or speech impairments, or English as an additional language
- Consider the challenges of distributing accessible and equitable topics across large cohorts
- Use clear structure, expectations, and advance materials to support preparation
- Acknowledge that live marking can be difficult and consider pre-recorded formats where appropriate

Flexible participation approaches:

- Offer multiple ways to contribute, including written, audio, or pre-recorded input alongside live discussion
- Provide options that reduce pressure on spontaneous verbal participation

Reasonable adjustments:

- Allow adjustments to pace and format (e.g. additional time, smaller groups)
- Use blended formats combining live and recorded elements

### Presentation

#### What is it?

An assessment in which students communicate ideas orally, often supported by visual or digital artefacts (e.g. slides, posters, demonstrations), and may be delivered live, recorded, individually, or in groups.

#### What's it for?

- To assess students' ability to communicate disciplinary knowledge and construct and defend arguments
- To assess students' ability to adapt content for a specific audience often drawing on application, synthesis, and evaluative judgement

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Presentations are particularly susceptible to hidden GenAI use unless AI engagement is either meaningfully restricted (Lane 1) or explicitly designed in and assessed (Lane 2).
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Use live, in‑person delivery with time limits
- Where group presentations are used, include mechanisms to evidence individual contribution
- Assess reasoning, explanation, and responsiveness, not slide polish alone
- Limit or clearly specify permitted AI use in preparation
- Require students to respond to unseen questions or prompts, or include a Q&A component

###### Lane 1 Example

An in‑class individual presentation where students explain and defend a disciplinary argument, followed by examiner questions to probe understanding and independent reasoning.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Explicitly incorporate GenAI into preparation or development stages
- Focus marking criteria on argument, interpretation, and evaluative decision‑making
- Include a reflective or justificatory element addressing the role and limits of GenAI
- Assess the quality of judgement, critique, or adaptation of AI‑supported content
- Make students' AI use transparent and accountable

###### Lane 2 Example

A presentation where students use GenAI to support brainstorming or structuring content, then critically refine and deliver an argument, accompanied by a short explanation of how AI informed (and did not replace) their thinking.

#### Authenticity

Students demonstrate communication skills, alongside core subject knowledge. Presentations skills are useful in future employment. Can be individual or collaborative and provide opportunities for peer-assessment and self-reflection. These skills can help students work towards the graduate skill of: Authenticity is strongest when presentations privilege disciplinary reasoning and judgement over polished delivery.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Presentations can introduce accessibility challenges related to anxiety, language, disability, or group dynamics.

Inclusive design strategies:

- Use clear criteria that prioritise content over performance style
- Offer alternative formats (e.g. recorded or support
- Structure group roles to support equitable participation
- Make expectations transparent and explicit

Reasonable adjustments:

- Provide adjusted delivery modes where needed
- Enable the use of assistive technologies
- Offer alternative assessment options

Programme-level considerations:

- Anticipate adjustments in advance
- Embed provision within assessment planning
- Avoid creating inequitable pressure on oral performance

### Viva Voce

#### What is it?

An assessment in which students respond orally to questions from one or more examiners, demonstrating their knowledge, understanding, and reasoning in real time. Vivas may be structured or semi-structured, conducted in person or online, and can be used as stand-alone assessments or in conjunction with other work (e.g. dissertations, projects, or portfolios).

#### What's it for?

- To assess students' depth of understanding and ability to articulate disciplinary knowledge
- To provide secure evidence of individual achievement and authorship
- To assess students' ability to justify decisions, interpretations, or approaches
- To evaluate reasoning, critical thinking, and the ability to respond to probing questions

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Vivas are inherently robust in contexts where independent thinking must be demonstrated, as they require real-time responses to questioning. They can function as strongly controlled assessments (Lane 1), or as opportunities to critically engage with AI-supported work (Lane 2), depending on how they are designed.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Use unseen or follow-up questions to probe independent understanding
- Use structured questioning to ensure consistency and fairness
- Focus on reasoning, explanation, and responsiveness
- Restrict or clearly define permitted AI use in preparation
- Conduct live, time-limited oral examination with examiners

###### Lane 1 Example

A live oral examination in which a student responds to structured and follow-up questions about their dissertation, demonstrating understanding, defending their argument, and evidencing independent reasoning under examiner questioning.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Explicitly incorporate discussion of AI-supported work or outputs
- Focus marking criteria on critical engagement and disciplinary reasoning
- Include reflective elements on the strengths and limitations of GenAI
- Assess evaluative judgement in interpreting or refining AI-generated content
- Require students to explain, critique, or justify their use of GenAI

###### Lane 2 Example

A viva linked to a coursework submission where students discuss how they used GenAI tools in their research or writing process, critically evaluating outputs and explaining how they refined them to meet disciplinary standards.

#### Authenticity

Vivas provide a direct and authentic way for students to demonstrate their knowledge, reasoning, and communication skills. They are particularly valuable for verifying authorship and understanding, and for assessing higher-order thinking in context. The format mirrors professional practices where individuals must explain, justify, and defend their thinking. Authenticity is strongest when questioning focuses on disciplinary reasoning, judgement, and the ability to engage critically and responsively.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Vivas can present accessibility challenges related to anxiety, communication style, disability, or processing speed.

Inclusive design strategies:

- Provide clear advance information about format, structure, and expectations
- Offer opportunities for familiarisation or practice with the format
- Use structured questioning to support clarity and reduce ambiguity
- Ensure consistency and sensitivity in examiner conduct

Reasonable adjustments:

- Allow additional time for responses where appropriate
- Offer rest breaks during longer assessments
- Enable alternative communication methods where needed
- Support the use of assistive technologies

## 6. Practical based

Assessment types in this category:

- OSCEs (Objective Structured Clinical Examinations)/ISCEs (Integrated Structured Clinical Examinations)

### OSCEs (Objective Structured Clinical Examinations)/ISCEs (Integrated Structured Clinical Examinations)

#### What is it?

In‑person (often clinical) assessments in which students rotate through timed stations to demonstrate defined skills, reasoning, and decision‑making, often using simulated patients, scenarios, or tasks aligned to professional practice.

#### What's it for?

- Assessing applied clinical competence
- Required prior to progression or accreditation
- Used where safe and effective practice must be demonstrated
- Examining clinical reasoning
- Measuring procedural performance
- Assessing communication abilities
- Evaluating professional skills

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

OSCEs/ISCEs are inherently AI‑resilient during delivery, but opportunities for designed, ethical GenAI use sit mainly in preparation, reflection, or debrief activities rather than live performance.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Use in‑person, supervised delivery with standardised stations and timing
- Avoid over‑fragmentation that reduces integrated judgement
- Focus assessment on performance, reasoning, communication, and professionalism
- Use trained examiners, clear marking rubrics, and moderation procedures
- Align stations explicitly to programme‑level learning outcomes and professional standards

###### Lane 1 Example

A multi‑station OSCE where students independently demonstrate history‑taking, clinical reasoning, and decision‑making in timed scenarios with simulated patients, assessed using standardised criteria.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Integrate GenAI outside the live OSCE as part of structured preparation or reflective learning
- Assess students' judgement about when and how AI is appropriate in clinical contexts
- Require students to critically evaluate AI outputs, identifying limitations, bias, or risk
- Scaffold AI use for activities such as scenario rehearsal, feedback generation, or differential diagnosis exploration

#### Authenticity

OSCEs/ISCEs are often viewed as highly authentic because they assess performance in simulated practice contexts; however, authenticity depends on integration and coherence. Authenticity is strongest when tasks require students to integrate knowledge, skills, and professional reasoning under constraint.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

OSCEs/ISCEs can present accessibility challenges related to anxiety, communication style, disability, or processing speed.

Inclusive design strategies:

- Provide clear advance information about format and expectations
- Ensure realistic and appropriate timing for tasks
- Build in flexibility in delivery where appropriate

Reasonable adjustments:

- Allow additional time where needed
- Offer rest breaks between stations
- Enable alternative communication methods
- Support the use of assistive technologies

## 7. Written Assessments

Assessment types in this category:

- Essay
- Report

### Essay

#### What is it?

An assessment in which students produce a structured written response to a question or prompt, drawing on relevant sources, theories, and evidence to construct and support an argument. Essays may vary in length and formality and are typically completed independently over a defined period, either under timed conditions or as coursework.

#### What's it for?

- Assessing students' ability to construct and communicate coherent arguments in writing
- Developing academic writing, reasoning, and evaluative judgement
- Assessing critical analysis, interpretation, and synthesis of sources
- Evaluating understanding and application of disciplinary knowledge

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Essays are particularly susceptible to hidden GenAI use unless tasks are either tightly controlled (Lane 1) or explicitly designed to incorporate and critically engage with AI (Lane 2). Clear expectations and assessment design are essential to ensure validity.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Use timed, in-person or controlled conditions where appropriate
- Focus assessment on argument, reasoning, and use of evidence rather than fluency alone
- Require use of specific or provided sources
- Restrict or clearly define permitted AI use
- Set unseen or tightly framed questions

###### Lane 1 Example

A timed, invigilated essay in which students respond to an unseen question, drawing on their knowledge to construct a structured argument and demonstrate independent critical thinking under controlled conditions.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Explicitly incorporate GenAI into research, drafting, or idea generation
- Focus marking criteria on interpretation, judgement, and originality of thinking
- Include reflective or justificatory commentary on process
- Assess students' ability to critique, refine, and build on AI outputs
- Require transparency about how AI tools were used

###### Lane 2 Example

An essay where students may use GenAI to support idea generation or drafting, but must submit a reflective commentary explaining how AI was used, critically evaluating outputs and demonstrating how they developed their own argument.

#### Authenticity

Essays support the development of core academic skills, including constructing arguments, engaging critically with sources, and communicating complex ideas effectively in writing. These skills are transferable across many professional contexts. Authenticity is strongest when essays prioritise disciplinary reasoning, evidence use, and critical judgement over surface-level fluency.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Essays can present accessibility challenges related to writing fluency, language, time management, or hidden expectations about academic conventions.

Inclusive design strategies:

- Provide clear guidance on structure, expectations, and marking criteria
- Use transparent and well-scaffolded prompts
- Ensure manageable scope and workload
- Offer exemplars or models where appropriate

Reasonable adjustments:

- Enable the use of assistive technologies (e.g. speech-to-text)

### Report

#### What is it?

An assessment in which students produce a structured written document addressing a specific task, problem, or scenario, often using disciplinary conventions such as headings, sections, data presentation, and evidence-based recommendations. Reports are typically analytical and applied in focus, and may be based on case studies, research, professional scenarios, or practical investigations.

#### What's it for?

- Assessing students' ability to apply disciplinary knowledge to real or simulated contexts
- Developing skills in making evidence-based recommendations or decisions
- Assessing professional and structured written communication
- Evaluating analytical thinking, problem-solving, and interpretation of data or evidence

#### Two-Lane Alignment

##### AI Considerations

Reports are vulnerable to hidden GenAI use where tasks can be completed through generic synthesis or formatting unless design either restricts AI use (Lane 1) or explicitly incorporates and evaluates it (Lane 2). Authenticity is strengthened through context-specific tasks and clear expectations.
- Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
- Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
- How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
- If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
- Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

##### Lane 1

###### How to make this Lane 1

- Use context-specific, applied, or localised scenarios that are difficult to replicate generically
- Focus assessment on analysis, interpretation, and justification rather than surface structure
- Require evidence of process, calculation, or methodology
- Restrict or clearly define permitted AI use
- Provide unique datasets, case materials, or prompts

###### Lane 1 Example

A report based on a provided dataset or case study, where students analyse findings and produce evidence-based recommendations, demonstrating their reasoning and methodology using materials not available outside the assessment.

##### Lane 2

###### How to make this Lane 2

- Explicitly allow GenAI use in drafting, structuring, or data interpretation
- Focus marking criteria on judgement, decision-making, and contextual appropriateness
- Include reflective commentary on strengths and limitations of AI support
- Assess critical evaluation and refinement of AI-generated outputs
- Require students to document and explain how AI tools were used

###### Lane 2 Example

A professional report where students may use GenAI to support structuring or initial drafting, but must critically evaluate outputs, adapt them to the context, and include a commentary explaining how AI contributed to and was refined within their work.

#### Authenticity

Reports closely mirror professional practices across many disciplines, requiring students to analyse information, communicate findings clearly, and make justified recommendations. This makes them highly authentic assessments. Authenticity is strongest when tasks reflect realistic contexts and prioritise interpretation, judgement, and decision-making over generic structure or formatting.

#### Inclusive Design and Reasonable Adjustments

Reports can present accessibility challenges related to structuring extended writing, interpreting task requirements, or managing complex information and data.

Inclusive design strategies:

- Provide clear guidance on structure, format, and expectations
- Use transparent marking criteria focused on analysis and reasoning
- Scaffold tasks with staged submissions or checkpoints where appropriate
- Ensure manageable scope and clarity in task requirements
- Where group work is involved (e.g. collaborative research reports), carefully plan how students are allocated to groups and how groups are managed, taking individual needs into consideration

Reasonable adjustments:

- Allow extended deadlines or additional time where appropriate
- Enable use of assistive technologies (e.g. text-to-speech, formatting tools)
- Offer alternative formats where needed
