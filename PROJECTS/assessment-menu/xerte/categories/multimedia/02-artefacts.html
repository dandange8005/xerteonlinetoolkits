What is it?

Artefact-based assessment involves students creating tangible or digital outputs that demonstrate their understanding, skills, and application of knowledge. These outputs might include sculptures, paintings, architectural designs, engineering prototypes, or other designed objects. Artefacts often combine conceptual thinking with practical execution, requiring students to synthesise ideas, techniques, and disciplinary knowledge into a coherent product. This type of assessment emphasises process as well as outcome, encouraging iterative development, reflection, and problem-solving.

What's it for?

  • Assessing applied, practice-based learning and technical skills
  • Supporting portfolio-building for future employment or study
  • Developing problem-solving and iterative design processes
  • Enabling students to demonstrate knowledge through making and doing
  • Encouraging creativity, innovation and design thinking

AI considerations

Producing artefacts can now be susceptible to the misuse of generative AI; encouraging students to demonstrate their ability to use AI tools effectively and critically, and to generate creative solutions can be supported by staff.

  • Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
  • Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
  • How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
  • If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
  • Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

Lane 1 and Lane 2

Lane 1

Secure assurance of learning

  • Require artefact production to take place in a supervised or observed setting (e.g., studio, lab, workshop)
  • Provide structured guidance during observed sessions
  • Focus assessment on both the process (observed) and the final product
  • Incorporate viva-style questioning or discussion alongside artefact creation
  • Limit or control access to external tools during observed periods (including AI where appropriate)
  • Use checkpoints where students explain decisions or processes in person
  • Include real-time demonstrations of skills and techniques
  • Break the task into live or timetabled sessions where progress is monitored
Example

In a computer science module, students are required to develop a simple software application during timetabled lab sessions. The task is completed in a supervised environment where students write code using specified tools and platforms. Across a series of structured sessions, they build the application step by step (eg. interface design, core functionality, testing).

Tutors observe students as they work, checking their coding practices, asking questions about their logic, and supporting debugging in real time. Students may also be asked to explain sections of their code or modify it live to demonstrate understanding. The final artefact is assessed alongside the observed development process and a brief in-person explanation of design choices.

This observed approach ensures that the submitted artefact reflects the student’s own technical ability and problem-solving skills, while allowing tutors to verify understanding as it develops.

Lane 2

Open assessment for and as learning

  • Set an artefact brief to be completed independently outside of supervised sessions
  • Focus assessment on final artefact quality, creativity, and evidenced process
  • Allow appropriate and transparent use of digital tools, including AI where relevant
  • Use process evidence to support academic integrity (rather than observation)
  • Incorporate checkpoints such as draft submissions or progress uploads
  • Require submission of a reflective commentary or design rationale
  • Encourage students to document their process (e.g., photos, videos, journals, portfolios)
  • Allow flexibility in when and where the artefact is produced
Example

In an architecture module, students design and produce a scale model or digital prototype independently over several weeks. They work off-site and at their own pace, using a range of tools and materials. To support the process, they submit a digital portfolio documenting sketches, iterations, and development stages, alongside a written rationale explaining their design decisions. The final artefact is assessed in combination with this supporting evidence. This approach allows flexibility and creative freedom while still ensuring that the student’s work can be authenticated through their documented process and reflection.

Practical considerations New field

Content needed: rooms and lab space, staff time and marking load at scale, timetabling, and resourcing adjustments.

Authenticity

Artefact-based assessment offers strong authenticity by mirroring the kinds of outputs and processes used in professional practice. Students engage in activities such as producing sculptures, paintings, architectural designs, and engineering models, which reflect real-world disciplinary expectations. This prepares students for practical applications beyond the classroom and helps them develop transferable skills such as design thinking, technical execution, and creative problem-solving. It also contributes to the development of the graduate attribute of being innovative, enterprising, and commercially aware, as students learn to produce work that has potential value in professional or public contexts.

Inclusive design and reasonable adjustments

Inclusive design for artefact-based assessment involves anticipating a wide range of student needs, abilities, and resources when setting tasks. While making and designing can be highly engaging, it can also present barriers related to access to materials, space, or technical skills.

Some considerations include:

  • Providing alternative formats for artefact submission (e.g., physical or digital versions)
  • Ensuring access to necessary materials, tools, or software
  • Offering training or support for required technical skills
  • Allowing flexibility in scale, complexity, or medium
  • Supporting varied levels of digital literacy through guidance and resources
  • Using inclusive assessment criteria that value process as well as final product
  • Providing clear instructions and exemplars
  • Incorporating assistive technologies where appropriate
  • Allowing additional time or adapted processes where needed
  • Encouraging collaborative or supported approaches where appropriate

Digital literacy can vary, so tasks requiring advanced technology may create accessibility challenges. However, assistive and creative technologies can also enhance participation and open up new possibilities for learners, supporting more inclusive and accessible engagement for all.
