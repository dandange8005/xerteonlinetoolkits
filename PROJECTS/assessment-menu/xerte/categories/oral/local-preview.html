Assessed seminars

Usually Lane 1 (live contributions)Lane 2 for assessed preparation

To resolve: If both the preparation and the live discussion are assessed, classify each part.

What is it?

Students’ individual contributions to a seminar are assessed, including interactions with peers, and evidence of preparation, as well as the quality of arguments.

What's it for?

  • Evaluating students’ ability to engage critically and collaboratively with course material
  • Supporting learning through dialogue and exposure to diverse perspectives
  • Promoting consistent preparation and engagement with seminar readings and topics
  • Encouraging active listening and constructive interaction with peers
  • Developing and evidencing skills in argumentation, reasoning, and defending ideas
  • Assessing communication skills, including clarity, confidence, and responsiveness in discussion

AI considerations

Assessed seminars align with Lane 1 when they require students to demonstrate their knowledge, reasoning, and communication skills in real time, under conditions that ensure the work is their own. Students may rely on genAI in a remote setting to create pre-prepared scripts on a given topic ahead of the assessment.

  • Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
  • Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
  • How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
  • If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
  • Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

Lane 1 and Lane 2

Lane 1

Secure assurance of learning

  • Keep assessment live and in-class (or synchronous)
  • Assess contributions made in real time during the seminar
  • Use follow-up prompts to probe depth of understanding
  • Ensure each student contributes verbally or interactively
  • Use structured turn-taking, small-group reporting, or directed questions
  • Make clear that contributions should reflect students’ own reasoning in the moment
  • If preparation is allowed, ensure assessment is based on live performance
Example

Students participate in a live, in-person seminar where they are assessed on their contributions to a structured academic discussion. Each student is expected to respond to prompts, engage with peers’ ideas, and articulate and defend their arguments in real time. The tutor may ask follow-up questions to probe understanding and verify reasoning, with marks awarded for clarity, use of evidence, and responsiveness within the session.

Lane 2

Open assessment for and as learning

  • Allow and scaffold the use of AI tools as part of preparation (e.g. idea generation, summarising readings, developing arguments)
  • Use seminars as a space to test, refine, and extend ideas developed outside class
  • Make expectations explicit around ethical and effective AI use
  • Incorporate collaborative activities where students build on and critique ideas (including those generated with AI)
  • Encourage students to bring AI-supported notes or prompts into the discussion
  • Assess the process as well as the contribution, including preparation, development of ideas, and critical engagement
  • Include pre- or post-seminar tasks where students reflect on how they used AI
Example

Students prepare for the seminar by using a range of resources, including generative AI tools, to explore key topics and develop initial arguments. During the session, they engage in discussion, drawing on their preparation to contribute ideas, respond to peers, and refine their thinking collaboratively. Assessment considers both the quality of their contributions and their ability to critically engage with and reflect on how their ideas were developed, including the use of AI in their preparation.

Practical considerations

Content needed: rooms and lab space, staff time and marking load at scale, timetabling, and resourcing adjustments.

Authenticity

Seminars develop students’ communication skills and the ability to answer questions through group discussion. It provides opportunities for students to demonstrate in-depth knowledge on an element of the curriculum. Seminars help develop students to work towards the graduate attribute; ‘independent and critical thinkers’.

Inclusive design and reasonable adjustments

Inclusive design in oral seminar-style assessments helps ensure that all students can participate meaningfully, regardless of differences in communication style, confidence, or access needs. Anticipating reasonable adjustments and providing flexible modes of engagement can reduce barriers while maintaining the value of discussion-based learning.

Inclusive design considerations:

  • Recognise potential challenges for students with anxiety, autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, hearing or speech impairments, or English as an additional language
  • Consider the challenges of distributing accessible and equitable topics across large cohorts
  • Use clear structure, expectations, and advance materials to support preparation
  • Acknowledge that live marking can be difficult and consider pre-recorded formats where appropriate

Flexible participation approaches:

  • Offer multiple ways to contribute, including written, audio, or pre-recorded input alongside live discussion
  • Provide options that reduce pressure on spontaneous verbal participation

Reasonable adjustments:

  • Allow adjustments to pace and format (e.g. additional time, smaller groups)
  • Use blended formats combining live and recorded elements
  • Adapt delivery to support individual access needs

Presentation

Usually either laneLane 1 live with Q&A; Lane 2 pre-recorded

To resolve: Classify by the main assessed part, or classify each part separately.

What is it?

An assessment in which students communicate ideas orally, often supported by visual or digital artefacts (e.g. slides, posters, demonstrations), and may be delivered live, recorded, individually, or in groups.

What's it for?

  • To assess students’ ability to communicate disciplinary knowledge and construct and defend arguments
  • To assess students’ ability to adapt content for a specific audience often drawing on application, synthesis, and evaluative judgement

AI considerations

Presentations are particularly susceptible to hidden GenAI use unless AI engagement is either meaningfully restricted (Lane 1) or explicitly designed in and assessed (Lane 2).

  • Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
  • Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
  • How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
  • If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
  • Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

Lane 1 and Lane 2

Lane 1

Secure assurance of learning

  • Use live, in‑person delivery with time limits
  • Where group presentations are used, include mechanisms to evidence individual contribution
  • Assess reasoning, explanation, and responsiveness, not slide polish alone
  • Limit or clearly specify permitted AI use in preparation
  • Require students to respond to unseen questions or prompts, or include a Q&A component
Example

An in‑class individual presentation where students explain and defend a disciplinary argument, followed by examiner questions to probe understanding and independent reasoning.

Lane 2

Open assessment for and as learning

  • Explicitly incorporate GenAI into preparation or development stages
  • Focus marking criteria on argument, interpretation, and evaluative decision‑making
  • Include a reflective or justificatory element addressing the role and limits of GenAI
  • Assess the quality of judgement, critique, or adaptation of AI‑supported content
  • Make students’ AI use transparent and accountable
Example

A presentation where students use GenAI to support brainstorming or structuring content, then critically refine and deliver an argument, accompanied by a short explanation of how AI informed (and did not replace) their thinking.

Practical considerations

Content needed: rooms and lab space, staff time and marking load at scale, timetabling, and resourcing adjustments.

Authenticity

Students demonstrate communication skills, alongside core subject knowledge. Presentations skills are useful in future employment. Can be individual or collaborative and provide opportunities for peer-assessment and self-reflection. These skills can help students work towards the graduate skill of: Authenticity is strongest when presentations privilege disciplinary reasoning and judgement over polished delivery.

Inclusive design and reasonable adjustments

Presentations can introduce accessibility challenges related to anxiety, language, disability, or group dynamics.

Inclusive design strategies:

  • Use clear criteria that prioritise content over performance style
  • Offer alternative formats (e.g. recorded or support
  • Structure group roles to support equitable participation
  • Make expectations transparent and explicit

Reasonable adjustments:

  • Provide adjusted delivery modes where needed
  • Enable the use of assistive technologies
  • Offer alternative assessment options

Programme-level considerations:

  • Anticipate adjustments in advance
  • Embed provision within assessment planning
  • Avoid creating inequitable pressure on oral performance

Viva voce

Usually Lane 1Not normally Lane 2

To resolve: The current menu offers a Lane 2 version. A viva is usually the verification step for Lane 2 work.

What is it?

An assessment in which students respond orally to questions from one or more examiners, demonstrating their knowledge, understanding, and reasoning in real time. Vivas may be structured or semi-structured, conducted in person or online, and can be used as stand-alone assessments or in conjunction with other work (e.g. dissertations, projects, or portfolios).

What's it for?

  • To assess students’ depth of understanding and ability to articulate disciplinary knowledge
  • To provide secure evidence of individual achievement and authorship
  • To assess students’ ability to justify decisions, interpretations, or approaches
  • To evaluate reasoning, critical thinking, and the ability to respond to probing questions

AI considerations

Vivas are inherently robust in contexts where independent thinking must be demonstrated, as they require real-time responses to questioning. They can function as strongly controlled assessments (Lane 1), or as opportunities to critically engage with AI-supported work (Lane 2), depending on how they are designed.

  • Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
  • Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
  • How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
  • If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
  • Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

Lane 1 and Lane 2

Lane 1

Secure assurance of learning

  • Use unseen or follow-up questions to probe independent understanding
  • Use structured questioning to ensure consistency and fairness
  • Focus on reasoning, explanation, and responsiveness
  • Restrict or clearly define permitted AI use in preparation
  • Conduct live, time-limited oral examination with examiners
Example

A live oral examination in which a student responds to structured and follow-up questions about their dissertation, demonstrating understanding, defending their argument, and evidencing independent reasoning under examiner questioning.

Lane 2

Open assessment for and as learning

Not normally Lane 2. This type happens under controlled, in-person conditions, so it suits Lane 1. To build in open learning, add a separate Lane 2 task, such as preparation or reflection before the assessment.

Practical considerations

Content needed: rooms and lab space, staff time and marking load at scale, timetabling, and resourcing adjustments.

Authenticity

Vivas provide a direct and authentic way for students to demonstrate their knowledge, reasoning, and communication skills. They are particularly valuable for verifying authorship and understanding, and for assessing higher-order thinking in context. The format mirrors professional practices where individuals must explain, justify, and defend their thinking. Authenticity is strongest when questioning focuses on disciplinary reasoning, judgement, and the ability to engage critically and responsively.

Inclusive design and reasonable adjustments

Vivas can present accessibility challenges related to anxiety, communication style, disability, or processing speed.

Inclusive design strategies

  • Provide clear advance information about format, structure, and expectations
  • Offer opportunities for familiarisation or practice with the format
  • Use structured questioning to support clarity and reduce ambiguity
  • Ensure consistency and sensitivity in examiner conduct

Reasonable adjustments

  • Allow additional time for responses where appropriate
  • Offer rest breaks during longer assessments
  • Enable alternative communication methods where needed
  • Support the use of assistive technologies
