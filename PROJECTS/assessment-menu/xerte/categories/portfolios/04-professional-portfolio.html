What is it?

A professional portfolio is a curated collection of work that demonstrates a student’s skills, knowledge, and development over time. It may include artefacts such as project work, reflections, case studies, presentations, or evidence from placements, typically accompanied by commentary that explains the context, learning, and progression.

What's it for?

  • Demonstrating applied skills and professional competencies
  • Promotes self-directed learning and goal setting
  • Builds a body of work that can be used for employment or further study
  • Supports integration of theory and practice
  • Encouraging reflection on learning and development over time

AI considerations

Professional portfolios can operate across both lanes, depending on how they are designed and assessed, balancing authentic, open-ended learning with opportunities to verify individual achievement.

There is significant potential for students to use AI tools to support drafting, reflection, or presentation within a portfolio. This introduces a risk that outputs may not fully represent the student’s own understanding or experience. However, AI can also support learning by helping students organise ideas, improve clarity, or reflect more deeply on their work. It is therefore important to design portfolio tasks that emphasise personalisation, critical reflection, and clear links to individual experience, ensuring that students demonstrate ownership of their work and decision-making processes.

  • Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
  • Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
  • How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
  • If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
  • Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

Lane 1 and Lane 2

Lane 1

Secure assurance of learning

  • Include in-person or supervised reflective discussions (e.g. viva, presentation, or portfolio defence)
  • Use targeted questioning to verify authenticity and depth of understanding
  • Focus assessment on personal experience, decision-making, and reasoning
  • Require students to explain and justify their artefacts and learning in real time
Example

Students submit a professional portfolio and participate in a short, live oral discussion where they explain selected artefacts, reflect on their learning, and respond to questions from the assessor. Marks are awarded based on the quality of the portfolio and the student’s ability to articulate and justify their work in real time.

Lane 2

Open assessment for and as learning

  • Allow AI use to support drafting, structuring, and reflection
  • Ask students to demonstrate progression over time and connect artefacts
  • Emphasise personalisation, synthesis, and integration of experiences
  • Include a reflective commentary on how tools (including AI) were used
  • Require students to critically evaluate and refine AI-supported outputs
Example

Students develop a professional portfolio over the course of a module, drawing on coursework, practical experiences, and reflections. They may use AI tools to help organise and draft reflective commentary but are required to critically review and personalise their work, including a reflection on how their portfolio represents their development and how any tools were used in its creation. Assessment focuses on the coherence, depth, and authenticity of the portfolio.

Practical considerations

Content needed: rooms and lab space, staff time and marking load at scale, timetabling, and resourcing adjustments.

Authenticity

Authenticity can be supported by requiring students to base their portfolio on personal experiences, original work, and individual development. This may include linking artefacts to specific learning activities, placements, or projects, and explaining the rationale behind their inclusion. Reflective commentary that connects experiences, demonstrates progression, and articulates learning helps foreground the student’s own voice. Incorporating elements such as oral defences, interim submissions, or links to other assessed work further strengthens confidence that the portfolio represents genuine student achievement.

Inclusive design and reasonable adjustments

Consider

  • Offering flexible formats (e.g. written, audio, video, or multimodal portfolios) to suit different strengths and needs
  • Providing clear scaffolding, such as templates, examples, and guiding questions to support structure and expectations
  • Allowing adjustments to timing, format, and tools, including assistive technologies and staged submissions to reduce cognitive load

Reasonable adjustments might include:

  • Extended deadlines
  • Alternative formats
  • Assistive technology use (eg. text-to-speech, speech-to-text technology).
