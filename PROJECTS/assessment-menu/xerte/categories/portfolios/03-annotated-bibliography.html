What is it?

A structured list of sources (such as books, journal articles, and websites), where each reference is accompanied by a short annotation. The annotation typically summarises the source, evaluates its credibility or relevance and explains how it relates to a particular topic or research question.

What's it for?

  • Developing research and information literacy skills
  • Promoting summarising, analysing, and synthesising information
  • Helping students build a foundation for essays, projects, or dissertations
  • Supporting understanding of academic conversations within a subject area
  • Encouraging critical evaluation of sources

AI considerations

There is significant potential for students to use AI tools to generate summaries or annotations of sources. This introduces a risk that learners may rely on AI without fully engaging with the original texts or developing their own evaluative skills. However, AI can also be used constructively to support understanding, for example by helping to clarify complex texts or suggest initial summaries. It is therefore important to design tasks that require critical engagement, verification of information, and clear demonstration of the student’s own interpretation.

  • Does GenAI risk undermining the intended learning outcome?
  • Can the work be confidently attributed to the student if not invigilated?
  • How susceptible is this task to AI misuse?
  • If AI is permitted, are expectations and transparency requirements clear?
  • Can a reflective or process‑evidence element reduce AI substitution?

Lane 1 and Lane 2

Lane 1

Secure assurance of learning

  • Include in-class elements or supervised tasks
  • Require links between annotations and taught content or discussions
  • Ask for evidence of reading (e.g., quotations, specific points)
Example

A Lane 1 annotated bibliography under controlled conditions might involve a student completing their annotations during a supervised session (e.g. in-class or timed assessment) where AI use is either restricted or explicitly disclosed and visible to the examiner. For example, the student could annotate a source immediately after an observed teaching activity, explaining how its ideas relate to what they actually did and what was observed, with any AI use (eg. for summarising the article) clearly documented in real time. This ensures the examiner can see both the student’s authentic thinking and exactly how AI was used, making the work verifiably grounded in personal, observed practice rather than generated content.

Lane 2

Open assessment for and as learning

  • Allow AI use for initial summarising or note-taking
  • Require students to check, refine, and critique AI-generated content
  • Include a reflective element on how AI was used
  • Emphasise synthesis, comparison, and originality in annotations
  • Ask students to identify inaccuracies or gaps in AI outputs
Example

A Lane 2 annotated bibliography example would involve a student independently producing annotations outside controlled conditions, where AI use may support tasks like summarising sources or identifying key arguments. For instance, a student might annotate a paper on formative assessment by summarising its main claims (potentially AI-assisted) and then critically evaluating its relevance to higher education practice. The quality of the work depends on how well the student engages with, critiques, and applies the literature, meaning that simply relying on AI-generated summaries would not achieve high marks without clear evidence of understanding and analysis.

Practical considerations

Content needed: rooms and lab space, staff time and marking load at scale, timetabling, and resourcing adjustments.

Authenticity

Authenticity can be supported by requiring students to demonstrate clear, traceable engagement with their chosen sources. This might include referencing specific arguments, using brief quotations, or linking sources directly to module content or assessment tasks. Asking students to explain why each source was selected, how it informed their understanding, or how it connects to their own research focus can help foreground their individual thinking. Incorporating reflective commentary or requiring links to subsequent coursework (e.g., essays or projects) further strengthens authenticity by situating the bibliography within the student’s own learning journey.

Inclusive design and reasonable adjustments

Annotated bibliographies can be flexibly designed to support diverse learners. Consider:

  • Offering a choice of sources and formats (eg. written, audio, or video annotations), or annotation length to suit different needs and preferences.
  • Providing clear scaffolding such as templates, exemplars, and guided questions to reduce cognitive load and support students unfamiliar with academic conventions.
  • Allowing the use of digital tools, including AI for initial comprehension support, to enhance accessibility, providing that expectations around critical engagement and academic integrity are clearly communicated.

Reasonable adjustments might include:

  • Extended deadlines
  • Alternative formats
  • Assistive technology use (eg. text-to-speech, speech-to-text technology).
